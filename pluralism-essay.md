# Pluralism: Good Because of the Bad Parts

In 2024, the Senate passed the Kids Online Safety Act 91 to 3. That almost never happens. The bill passed because parents whose kids were hurt by social media, along with a lot of young people, organized and kept showing up until senators listened. Meta did not like the bill, so it spent a record $18.9 million on lobbying in just the first nine months of that year, and the House never voted on it (Issue One). Most people would look at that and say the system is broken. I think it shows the system working. Pluralism is good, and a lot of the time it is good because of the parts that look bad.

Pluralism is the idea that power in a democracy is spread out between a lot of competing groups instead of being held by one elite or one big majority (Dahl). Policy comes out of those groups fighting, bargaining, and compromising with each other. James Madison saw this coming. In Federalist 10 he said factions are dangerous but you can't get rid of them without getting rid of freedom, so the answer is a big republic with so many groups that none of them can take over. A large republic, he wrote, takes in "a greater variety of parties and interests" (Madison). The First Amendment backs this up by protecting the right "to petition the Government for a redress of grievances" (US Const., amend. I). Interest groups are not a glitch in the system. They are the system.

The first thing that looks bad is that interest groups are selfish. The NRA cares about guns, unions care about workers, and the American Medical Association cares about doctors. None of them are looking out for the whole country. But that is exactly why it works. Selfish groups fight each other, and when everybody is fighting, nobody gets to run everything. The second thing that looks bad is gridlock. It is frustrating when nothing gets passed, but gridlock is also what stops a majority from just steamrolling everyone else. Something only passes when enough different groups can live with it.

The biggest complaint about pluralism is money. E.E. Schattschneider said, "The flaw in the pluralist heaven is that the heavenly chorus sings with a strong upper-class accent" (Schattschneider 35). He has a point. A company like Meta can hire dozens of lobbyists, and a single mom working two jobs cannot. But even this has an upside. When one side has all the power, it gives everyone else something to organize against and fight for. Political scientist David Truman argued that this is how interest groups form in the first place: when one group gains power, the people it affects get organized in response (Truman). Unions exist because companies had all the power. The parents behind the Kids Online Safety Act got organized because a tech company was not going to protect their kids for them.

Some people say poor people don't have the time or money to organize, so they just lose. That's just not true. In 2012, a couple hundred fast-food workers in New York City walked off the job and demanded $15 an hour and the right to form a union (Rivas). People laughed at that number. Four years later, California and New York both signed laws raising their minimum wage to $15 (Ware). And when people really can't speak for themselves, advocates do it for them. Groups like the National Alliance to End Homelessness lobby for people who will never be able to afford a lobbyist (National Alliance to End Homelessness).

That doesn't mean everything is fine. If Madison saw what is going on today, I think he would say it has gotten way out of hand. The amount of money is ridiculous, but the bigger problem is the internet. His whole plan depended on groups actually running into each other, arguing, and being forced to make deals. Now people can live inside echo chambers where they only hear from their own side, and some groups just straight up deny reality. You can't bargain with someone who doesn't agree on what is true. When that happens, pluralism stops being a competition and turns into a bunch of groups yelling past each other.

So the problem isn't that there are too many interest groups or that some of them are rich. That stuff has always pushed other people to organize and fight back, and that is the system doing its job. The problem is when groups stop living in the same reality. Pluralism can handle selfish groups, rich groups, and gridlock. What it can't handle is people who won't even argue about the same facts.
