# Political Socialization and the Madisonian Model

Last Thanksgiving my uncle and my cousin spent twenty minutes arguing about the same bill and somehow were not talking about the same country. One of them had seen a clip on TikTok. The other had heard a segment on cable. Neither of them changed their mind, and both of them already knew which side they were on before dinner started. That fight is political socialization in real time. It is also a test of whether James Madison's design still fits the people living under it.

The Madisonian Model is Madison's answer to faction. In Federalist 10 he says the causes of faction are "sown in the nature of man," and that trying to remove them would mean destroying liberty, because "liberty is to faction what air is to fire." So the system does not try to make people agree. It tries to control the effects. A large republic takes in "a greater variety of parties and interests," which makes it harder for one majority to run over everyone else. Federalist 51 adds the structure. "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition," and the compound republic divides power twice: first between the national government and the states, then inside each government among separate departments. That is the double security. Madison assumed selfish people, not virtuous ones. The question is whether the way Americans actually learn politics still produces factions his system can handle.

Political socialization is the process by which people pick up political beliefs, values, and habits. The main agents are family, school, peers and groups, the media, and major events. Family is the strongest on party identification. School is supposed to teach the rules of the system. Media used to be a shared filter. Now it is mostly a personal one. Events like 9/11, the 2008 crash, COVID, and January 6 stamp a generation, but they do not stamp every generation the same way.

Family transmission and generational turnover still give Madison some of what he wanted. Algorithmic media and thin civic education detract from the model. They sort people into durable, fact-resistant factions that want to win outright instead of bargain. The extended republic and federalism can absorb disagreement. They have a harder time when the disagreement is about what happened.

The part that still adds to the model is the messiness of how people get their politics. Family is the strongest predictor of party ID, and that sounds like inherited tribalism. It is also how you get stable factions instead of a new majority every news cycle. Survey work in the ANES tradition finds that about 62 percent of people raised by parents who consistently voted Democratic identify as Democrats, and about 57 percent of people raised by consistent Republican parents identify as Republicans (Survey Center on American Life, 2024). That is inheritance, not a fax machine. A lot of people break from their parents, especially once gender, education, and region get involved. A Catholic union household in Ohio is not the same faction as a secular professional household in a coastal suburb, even if both lean the same way on a presidential ballot. Region, religion, and occupation still cut across party. That is closer to Madison's multiplicity of interests than a clean two-team map.

School and the older media system also socialized people into the rules, not just the teams. Civic classes and a shared evening newscast were not neutral, but they did teach that ambition is supposed to run through elections, courts, and Congress. The Washington Post's Watergate reporting is the clean example of ambition counteracting ambition. The press, acting in its own interest, checked the presidency, and a generation learned that the system has an outside brake. Federalism does related work. State and local fights over schools, policing, and taxes give people a smaller arena where a faction can win without capturing the whole republic. Madison counted on that vertical split. It still operates every time a state goes its own way on a policy the national parties are screaming about.

The detraction is newer, and it is mostly about information. Pew's 2025 News Platform Fact Sheet finds that 53 percent of U.S. adults at least sometimes get news from social media, and 43 percent of adults under 30 regularly get news on TikTok, up from 9 percent in 2020 (Pew Research Center, 2025a). That is not a shared gatekeeper. It is an algorithm trained on what you already stop for. A separate Pew study from June 2025 shows the split in trust. Fifty-six percent of Republicans trust Fox News, while 58 percent distrust CNN. Democrats are close to the reverse: 58 percent trust CNN, and 64 percent distrust Fox News (Pew Research Center, 2025b). Madison could live with people who disagreed about the tariff. He was writing for people who could still argue about the same event. When each party has its own trusted sources and actively distrusts the other party's sources, you get epistemic factions. They do not just want different policies. They do not grant the other side a common set of facts to bargain from.

That also undercuts the extended republic. Geographic sorting plus nationalized media means a lot of districts are fighting the same culture war instead of a local mix of interests. The decline of local news makes it worse. Federalism was supposed to multiply the issues so that an ally on one question is an opponent on the next. A national feed flattens that. Generational events do not fully repair it. 9/11, 2008, COVID, and January 6 created real divides by age, but they mostly hardened the parties people already inherited. If civic education is thin on top of that, citizens know their side and do not know what Congress actually does. They then ask a president to just get it done, which is a demand the Madisonian structure was built to resist.

Madison would not be shocked that people are selfish or partisan. He planned for that. He would be less comfortable with a public that inherits a team, then spends its political life inside a feed that confirms the team and treats the other feed as fake. The double security still exists on paper. It works better when socialization produces citizens who expect to lose sometimes and still stay inside the system. Right now it produces a lot of citizens who treat a loss as proof the system is rigged. If that is the political self most people bring to the Constitution, the model is still standing, but it is standing on a public it was not fully written for.
